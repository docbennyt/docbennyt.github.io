Your first developer portfolio needs one good project

A small, finished project gives you something concrete to discuss in an interview. Here’s what I’d build and what I’d put in the README.

By Benedictus Makuyana18 November 2026

In this article

  • Find a project you can finish
  • Make the work easy to inspect
  • Prepare to tell the story out loud
  • Sources

When someone asks what you can build, a project gives you a better starting point than a list of languages. You can open it, point to a decision, and explain what happened when that decision met an actual user. If I were starting again, I’d spend more time making one link worth clicking.

I’d pick a problem in reach. The idea behind CalenderZW fits in a sentence: turn a published campus timetable into a schedule you can use on your phone. That gives me something I can try with a real input and a real device. It also gives me an obvious first version. I can make one timetable useful before figuring out what every university publishes.

Find a project you can finish

Write down who will use it, what they start with, and what they should have in hand at the end. For a timetable tool, that might be a student with a copied timetable who wants to see tomorrow’s first lecture. A login screen and a settings page can wait. An empty result and a malformed row are worth handling early because they can stop that first student in their tracks.

Give yourself a concrete end point. For example, the app should accept a timetable with two sessions on the same day, display them in order, and let someone recover when they paste the wrong thing. Keep three sample inputs in the repo: one ordinary, one incomplete, and one that exposed a bug. They become your demo, your test data, and the raw material for a decent interview answer.

Make the work easy to inspect

GitHub puts a repository’s README where visitors can see it and suggests covering what the project does, why it’s useful, and how to get started. Put your working demo near the top. Give the reviewer a sample input they can use right away. If there’s a piece of code you’re especially proud of, link directly to the lines at a specific commit so the link stays useful as the file changes.

Add a short note on one decision. I’d like to read why you chose to ask for confirmation before exporting an ambiguous date, or why you dropped a feature after trying it on a small screen. Name your contribution if you worked with other people. A small, clearly attributed decision gives a reviewer something specific to ask about.

Prepare to tell the story out loud

Record yourself opening the project cold. In two minutes, show the problem, complete the flow, then show how the app responds to a bad input. If you need five minutes of setup or a private credential, fix the demo or provide a short recording alongside it. Keep setup instructions for the person who wants to run the code.

The next time someone asks how you handle feedback, you’ll have an answer you can show. Bring up the original behavior, explain what you changed, and point to the check you added. That’s the part of a first project I’d invest in.
