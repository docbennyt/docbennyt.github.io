What should a customer be able to do on your website?

Start with the question a customer came to answer. Then test whether they can finish the job from a phone.

By Benedictus Makuyana18 November 2026

#Product-Design#UX#Small-Business#Performance

In this article

  • Start with the customer's question
  • Design the next step
  • Test the whole journey on a phone
  • Sources

A website can look polished and still fail at the moment a customer tries to book. The page is not finished when it looks good on your laptop. It is finished when the visitor can do the job they came to do.

PRODUCT PRINCIPLE

Start with the customer's question. Choose the page content and call to action that help answer it.

1. Start with the customer's question

Imagine a traveller follows a guesthouse link from WhatsApp and wants to know whether a room is available for Friday. They may also need to know the price, parking arrangements and how a booking is confirmed. By the time they reach the website, they have already found the business. The page should make the decision easier.

Ask the owner for a few recent enquiries and remove personal details before reviewing them. Look for repeated questions. If guests keep asking whether parking is available, make that answer easy to find. If reception must confirm a room manually, say that an enquiry is not yet a confirmed booking.

2. Design the next step

01UnderstandWhat does this visitor need to know?
02ChooseCan they compare the relevant options?
03ContinueIs the next action and response clear?

Write one job for each important page. A room page helps a visitor judge whether it fits their group. An enquiry form collects dates and guest count, then explains when the team expects to reply. Sketch those actions before choosing a carousel or adding decorative sections.

3. Test the whole journey on a phone

Open the site on a small phone, use a throttled connection and complete one task with one hand. Can you read the room details without pinching? Does the contact link make it clear what will open? If the form rejects an entry, can you correct it without losing the rest?

WCAG 2.2's Reflow guidance uses a 320 CSS-pixel viewport for most vertically scrolling content. That is a useful check for fixed-width elements that looked harmless on a laptop. Also watch Core Web Vitals: LCP for loading, INP for responsiveness and CLS for unexpected layout shifts. They help diagnose experience problems, but they do not tell you whether the visitor actually booked.

Measure the handoff too. A tap on WhatsApp means someone opened a route to reception; it does not prove a room was booked. For a small business, start with a simple record of enquiries and outcomes, count each enquiry once and avoid collecting details the team does not need.

FIELD TEST

Run this before launch

  • Choose one real customer question from recent enquiries.
  • Complete the journey at 320 CSS pixels and on a slower connection.
  • Submit the form with one missing or invalid field.
  • Record whether the enquiry was answered, confirmed or still pending.
