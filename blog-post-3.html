What should a customer be able to do on your website?

Start with the question a customer came to answer. Then test whether they can finish the job from a phone.

By Benedictus Makuyana18 November 2026

In this article

  • Follow the customer’s next step
  • Measure visits and watch what happens after the click
  • Sources

Imagine you run a guesthouse. A traveller follows a link from WhatsApp and wants to know if they can book for Friday. By the time they see your site, they’ve already done the work of finding you. Now the page has one job: help them decide if they can stay and tell them what happens next.

I’d start a website brief by asking the owner for a handful of recent enquiries. Redact the personal details. Read the questions themselves. If guests keep asking whether parking is available, make it easy to find. If they can request rooms but somebody at reception has to check the booking, use wording that makes that sequence clear. The visitor should know whether to expect a confirmation or keep looking.

Follow the customer’s next step

Write down one job for each important page. The homepage could help a visitor choose a service. A room page could help them judge whether it fits their group. The enquiry form could collect the dates and number of guests, then tell them when the team expects to reply. You can sketch all three actions on paper in the time it takes to choose a carousel.

Open the website on the smallest phone you can find. Use a throttled connection and try to complete one task with one hand. Can you read the room details without pinching? Does the contact link show what it will open? If you make a mistake in the form, can you correct it without losing everything else? The W3C’s reflow guidance makes the width check concrete at 320 CSS pixels for most vertical content. It’s a good place to find fixed-width elements you missed on your laptop.

Measure visits and watch what happens after the click

Google’s Core Web Vitals measure loading, responsiveness, and visual stability through LCP, INP, and CLS. I’d use them to diagnose things like a room photo that loads late or a page that shifts under a thumb. A lab run helps reproduce problems under controlled conditions. Field data, where there’s enough of it, tells you more about the devices and connections people actually use.

Then check the handoff. A tap on WhatsApp means somebody opened a route to reception. It can’t tell you whether a room was booked. For a low-volume business I’d start with a small record of enquiries and outcomes, count each thread once, and avoid collecting information that the team doesn’t need. Looking at a few real cases beats trying to infer all of that from clicks.

A page I can hand to a customer with confidence answers the first question quickly. If they decide to proceed, they know which button to press and who is going to reply.
